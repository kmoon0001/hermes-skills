# Public Conflict Dossiers

Use this skill when the user needs a defensible, source-led report about conflicts of interest, credibility issues, publication motives, market/litigation amplification, or professional background of a public actor. Typical examples: short-seller publications, activist-investor reports, expert-witness credibility, industry attack pieces, law-firm solicitations, or public professional background checks.

## Operating principles

1. Public/professional only.
   - Use public filings, official pages, published articles, court/regulatory/law-firm notices, archived source pages, and the subject's own disclaimers.
   - Personal/private-life material: default EXCLUDE. Only include when the user EXPLICITLY requests it, the material is verifiably public (court records, published interviews, regulatory filings), and it directly bears on credibility or pattern of conduct. Flag with clear sourcing. Do not include gossip, unverified doxxing, or hearsay.
   - If useful but sensitive/off-record material appears, quarantine it separately and mark DO NOT PUBLISH.

2. Lead with the subject's own words.
   - Own disclosures and disclaimers are strongest: short/long position disclosures, affiliate/control disclosures, trading disclaimers, methodology caveats, as-is disclaimers, no-update language, business-model descriptions.
   - Treat third-party commentary as corroborating/contextual, not as primary proof.

3. Separate conflict from falsity.
   - Conflicts, incentives, and motive do not prove the challenged report is false.
   - Use safe language: disclosed conflict, financially conflicted publication model, requires independent verification, material credibility context.
   - Avoid unsupported legal conclusions: fraud, market manipulation, lied, unless quoting a source or presenting counsel-only issue spotting with explicit caveats.

4. Preserve adversarial usefulness without overclaiming.
   - Build counter-ammunition with quotes, dates, links, and source labels.
   - State what is documented, why it matters, and how to phrase it safely.
   - Include a what-not-to-say / what-not-found section to prevent overreach.

5. Verify every claim against its source.
   - MANDATORY: For every factual claim in the output, confirm you can cite the exact source URL and quote. If you cannot trace a claim back to a source, remove it or mark it as unverified.
   - After writing, audit the document by searching for every named entity and confirming the source table covers it.
   - User preference: one user explicitly rejected prior versions that lacked traceable citations. Default to over-citation, not under.

## Workflow

1. Define scope and boundary.
   - Who/what is being reviewed?
   - What publication/campaign/event is in scope?
   - Mark whether output is public-ready, counsel-review, or quarantine.

2. Collect source classes.
   - Primary subject sources: article/report, methodology, legal disclaimer, about/team pages, disclosure pages.
   - Regulatory/professional sources: SEC/IAPD/Form ADV, FINRA, licensing boards, court dockets, agency records.
   - Market/litigation amplification: investor alerts, class-action solicitations, law-firm pages, stock-movement coverage.
   - Third-party credibility coverage: reputable press, industry trade press, ethics commentary.
   - Target's rebuttal/counterpoints: company statements, analyst notes reported by media, official releases.

3. Extract evidence into a source ledger.
   - Capture source title, URL, date, source type, exact quote/paraphrase, and relevance.
   - Prefer exact quotes for core conflict language.
   - Record caveats: paywall, AI-generated article, opinion/editorial, advocacy release, legal advertisement, or partial extract.

4. Analyze by issue, not by source dump.
   - Financial conflict / position exposure.
   - Common control / affiliation.
   - Ability to trade before/after publication.
   - Litigation monetization or law-firm collaboration.
   - Methodology limitations and data caveats.
   - Disclaimers/no-guarantee language.
   - Market impact and amplification.
   - Third-party credibility concerns.
   - Rebuttal/counterpoints.
   - Regulatory/enforcement context, if any, with strict caveats.

5. Write the report in counsel-safe form.
   - Title, date, scope, boundary, status.
   - TL;DR bullets.
   - Source table.
   - Findings with: documented fact -> why it matters -> safe wording -> source.
   - Clean argument stack.
   - Recommended safe language.
   - Avoid-language / what not to claim.
   - What was not found.
   - Bottom line.

6. Verify the artifact.
   - Confirm file exists.
   - Search within it for key required sections and source names.
   - Make sure all loaded claims are either quoted, sourced, or explicitly caveated.

## Output tiers

When the user requests adversarial/counsel-level work, produce THREE tiers of output. This was validated on a short-seller counter-dossier (Hunterbrook vs Ensign, Jul 2026):

**Tier 1: Legal Defense Brief** (for lawyers)
- Daubert/FRE challenges to methodology
- Cross-examination outlines with numbered questions
- Discovery/document preservation plan
- Pattern-of-conduct evidence (FRE 404(b))
- SEC/regulatory defense strategy
- Potential expert witness list
- Motion to dismiss / defamation counterclaim analysis

**Tier 2: Research Dossier** (for the user/internal team)
- Source table with claim, evidence, URL, exact quote
- Numbered findings with documentation chain
- What-not-found section

**Tier 3: Layman Guide** (for PR, board, general consumption)
- Plain English — no jargon, no legal language
- 30-second answer the user can memorize
- One-liner for social media
- Analogies that make the flaw obvious (broken scale, worst-McDonalds-generalized)
- Follow-the-money section
- The questions the opponent cannot answer

## Short-seller track record research

When the opponent is a short-seller/activist-investor (not just a journalist), add a track-record section:

- List ALL companies they've targeted with dates
- For each: stock drop %, did stock recover, did company fight back, outcome
- Highlight failures: shorts that didn't stick, stocks that recovered, companies that successfully rebutted
- Check for: SEC whistleblower filings (stand to collect 30% of penalty), class-action coordination speed, undisclosed conflicts
- Source pattern: web_search for "[firm name] report [company] stock recovered/rebounded", check Tortoise Media/Reuters/WSJ for coverage

## Court-ready evidence chain table

For every substantive claim, produce a table like:

| Claim | Source | URL | Exact Quote |
|-------|--------|-----|-------------|
| ... | ... | ... | ... |

This is critical for counsel to cite in filings. Without this table, the document is commentary, not evidence.

## Cross-examination outline pattern

For litigation-prep dossiers, include a Q&A outline targeting:

1. Qualifications — No healthcare experience? No journalism training? First time doing this?
2. Methodology — Started from worst facility? Only 5 months? One formula? Admit estimates?
3. Financial motive — Short position disclosed? Class-action coordination? SEC whistleblower cut?
4. Track record — Previous targets that recovered? Companies that fought back? Disclosure violations?
5. Pattern evidence — Other undisclosed conflicts? Personal investments?

## Safe wording snippets

- "The disclosed conflict does not prove the report false, but it is material context for evaluating motive, timing, and market impact."
- "The report should be treated as financially conflicted advocacy requiring independent verification, not neutral fact-finding."
- "Public sources document a structural conflict: [source] disclosed [specific exposure/relationship]."
- "No adjudicated finding of wrongdoing was identified in the reviewed public sources."

## Pitfalls

- Do not confuse a law-firm investor alert with a filed lawsuit.
- Do not treat an opinion column as adjudicated fact.
- Do not call something regulatory misconduct merely because an entity is exempt/not registered; describe the exact regulatory posture.
- Do not infer a private relationship or motive when the public structural disclosure is sufficient.
- Do not bury the user's ammunition in long prose; put the strongest points in bullets and tables first.
